# 5. How to spot the ISS

## What it looks like
- A **bright, steady white dot**, often brighter than any star.
- It moves **smoothly and fast**, crossing the sky in about 2–6 minutes.
- **No blinking lights.** If it blinks, it's a plane.
- It doesn't make any sound.

## When you can see it
You can only see the ISS when:
1. **Your sky is dark** (about an hour before sunrise or after sunset is best), **and**
2. **The ISS is still in sunlight**, high above you. It doesn't have its own bright lights. It shines because the Sun reflects off its huge solar panels.

That's why most passes are marked **No** in the table: they happen in daylight, or in the middle of the night when the ISS is in Earth's shadow. The tracker works all of this out for you. Look for **👀 Yes**.

Sometimes the ISS **fades out in the middle of the sky**. That's the moment it flies into Earth's shadow, like a sunset for the space station.

## Where to look
The alert tells you where it appears ("Look NW"). Use your phone's compass app to find the direction.

**The fist trick for height:** hold your arm straight out and make a fist. One fist is about **10°**.
- **10–20°:** low, just above trees and roofs
- **40°:** halfway up
- **90°:** straight overhead

So "climbs to 40°" means about four fists above the horizon.

## Ratings and brightness
Every visible pass gets **1 to 5 stars** (⭐) in the passes table:
- **Brighter** passes get more stars. It's brightest when it's close (high in the sky) and when the Sun is behind you, lighting up the side you're looking at.
- **Higher** passes get more stars, because they're easier to see over trees and houses.
- **Longer** passes get a bit more.
- **Clouds** take stars away.

The brightness is written in words too:

| What the page says | Magnitude | Like… |
|---|---|---|
| Super bright, like Venus | −3 or brighter | The brightest thing in the night sky after the Moon |
| Very bright, brighter than any star | −1.5 to −3 | Jupiter |
| Bright, like the brightest stars | +0.5 to −1.5 | Sirius, Vega |
| Like an ordinary star | +0.5 to +2.5 | The stars of the Big Dipper |
| Faint, you need a dark sky | fainter than +2.5 | Hard to see from a city |

*Magnitude* is how astronomers measure brightness: the **smaller** the number, the **brighter** it is. The tracker's brightness is an estimate, often within about one magnitude.

## Use the sky chart
The **🧭 Sky chart** on the page shows the pass like a compass lying on the ground:
- The **edge of the circle is the horizon** and the **middle is straight overhead**. The rings are 30° and 60° up.
- The **green dot** is where it appears. The **bright line with the arrow** is the part you can see, and the arrow shows which way it moves.
- The **dashed part** is when it's up there but you can't see it (your sky is too bright, or it's in Earth's shadow).
- The **🌙 Moon and bright planets** are shown as well, so you can use them as landmarks ("it passes just above Jupiter").
- Tap any pass in the table to see its chart. During a pass, the 🛰️ on the chart shows where it is right now.

## Spotting Tiangong and Hubble
- **Tiangong** (China's space station) looks like a smaller ISS: a bright, steady dot, but a bit dimmer. Its path never goes farther than about 42° north or south of the equator, so it's easier to see the closer you live to the equator.
- **Hubble** is much smaller and looks like an ordinary star moving slowly and steadily. You need a dark sky away from streetlights. Hubble stays close to the equator (never farther than 28.5° north or south), so from far north or south it stays low in the sky or never rises at all.

## Spotting a Starlink train
Right after a SpaceX launch, a batch of Starlink satellites flies in a **line, like a train of lights**, one after another. It's an amazing sight, but only for the first few days to a week or two: after that they spread out and climb higher, and get much dimmer. The **✨ Starlink** button tracks the newest launch. If it says there's no train right now, check again after the next launch.

## Tips
- Go outside **2–3 minutes early** and let your eyes adjust.
- Higher passes (40° and up) are brighter and easier to see.
- Check the **Clouds** column. ☀️ Clear is great, ⛅ Partly cloudy is worth a try, and ☁️ Cloudy usually means you won't see it.
- **Wave!** There are people on board (see **Who's on board** on the page). 👋
